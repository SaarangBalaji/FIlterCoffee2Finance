The Hypothesis

Everyone, regardless of income, prefers investing in gold for contingency planning.

One of three founding questions behind this project. The other two: who actually holds the purse strings at home, and why festivals and weddings pull so hard on family budgets. This one is about gold specifically — not whether people like it, but whether it functions as a deliberate financial safety net, across every income bracket.

Why Chennai, why now

We don't have our own regional data yet — that's exactly what this project is going to produce. But the reason we chose to ask this question here, rather than assume the answer, isn't a hunch. It's sitting on top of some genuinely striking economics.

South India isn't just fond of gold — it dominates India's gold economy. According to the World Gold Council's own India market research, South India accounts for 40% of the country's entire gold jewellery demand, more than any other region, with Chennai named specifically as one of the region's key centres. A 2016 consumer study cited in the same WGC research found Southern India had the highest gold ownership rate of any region in the country (76% of respondents) and the highest rate of recent purchases (60% had bought gold jewellery in the past year alone). This isn't a stereotype — it's the documented centre of gravity of Indian gold demand. (World Gold Council, "Understanding regional, income and demographic differences")

Women's gold holdings, in aggregate, are a monetary-scale phenomenon, not just a household habit. By some widely cited World Gold Council–sourced estimates, Indian women collectively hold more gold than the combined official gold reserves of several of the world's largest sovereign holders. Whatever the precise figure in any given year, the scale itself is the point: what happens inside individual households, one gold chain and one wedding at a time, adds up to something large enough to matter at a national economic level. That's a big part of why H2 (who holds the purse strings, especially on gold) and H1 (why gold at all) are related questions, not separate ones.

And it isn't only households doing this. At the same time families have been quietly building up gold for generations, India's own central bank has been doing something structurally similar at the sovereign level. The Reserve Bank of India has been one of the world's most active central-bank gold buyers since the pandemic — adding roughly 244 tonnes between 2020 and 2024, crossing 880 tonnes in total reserves by 2025, and ranking as the second-largest sovereign gold buyer globally over that period, behind only China. Analysts consistently frame this the same way our respondents talk about their own gold: as a hedge against currency risk and geopolitical uncertainty, and a form of trust that doesn't depend on any single institution. (Business Standard; Trading Economics, India Gold Reserves)

Put together: the same behaviour we're asking a Chennai household about — buying gold as a deliberate hedge against an uncertain future — is also, right now, the RBI's own monetary strategy. If a central bank with access to every financial instrument on Earth still chooses to hold more gold for exactly this reason, the household-level instinct we're studying starts to look less like superstition and more like the same logic, applied at a smaller scale. That's what makes this worth testing properly rather than assuming.

One figure we came across a lot while researching this — that Tamil Nadu households alone hold several thousand tonnes of gold, more than entire countries like Germany or Italy hold in reserve — circulates widely online, but we couldn't trace it back to a primary World Gold Council report we could verify, so we're leaving it out rather than repeat an unverified number. If we find the original source, we'll add it.

The economics

This hypothesis sits inside behavioural economics, and it touches a handful of ideas that are more interesting than they first sound — each with real research behind it.

Precautionary saving

The classic version of this question: when the future is uncertain — an illness, a bad month, a lost job — people set money aside as a buffer. The foundational formal treatment is Miles Kimball's 1990 Econometrica paper, showing the strength of this motive can be measured with the same math economists use for risk aversion. What's fascinating here is testing whether, where formal insurance and credit are harder to access, gold becomes that buffer. (Kimball, 1990)

Mental accounting

A term coined by Nobel laureate Richard Thaler: people don't treat money as one interchangeable pool — they mentally sort it into separate "accounts," even when that's technically inefficient. Gold bought for a daughter's future wedding, never touched even under financial strain, is mental accounting in its purest form: not "savings," a promise kept in a physical object. (Thaler, 1999)

Risk aversion & asset choice

Gold pays no interest and no dividend — by cold financial logic, a worse place to park money than almost anything else available. So why does it keep winning? The behavioural answer is that people aren't only pricing return — they're pricing trust, volatility, and universal recognizability, and gold scores highly on all three, especially where trust in formal institutions is thin.

Household finance & access

The honest complication: is gold ownership purely about preference, or does it depend on simply having enough spare income to buy any of it in the first place? John Campbell's 2006 presidential address to the American Finance Association put the "limited participation puzzle" on the map — people can be priced out of an asset class they'd otherwise choose. Untangling preference from access is a big part of what this hypothesis is testing. (Campbell, 2006)

Our methodology

The exact wording changes by who we're talking to — a factory-floor household, an auto driver, or a business owner get different instruments — but the underlying question stays the same. A few examples, verbatim:

"Many families buy gold regularly. Why do you think gold is preferred over stocks or mutual funds?"

"Do you trust banks more, or do you trust gold more?"

"Does your household own or regularly buy gold? What is the main reason for keeping it?"

"Suppose you had ₹100,000 that you would not need immediately. Where would you feel safest keeping it — gold, a bank deposit, cash, or somewhere else? Why?"

"If someone gave you ₹1 lakh today as a gift, what would you do with it?"

"When your family thinks about preserving wealth, what feels most reassuring or culturally natural — gold, property, business, market investments, or a mix — and why?"

Every interview is recorded, transcribed, and coded before it becomes a data point — see "How We Work" on the Research page for the full process.

What our respondents say

— Muthu, auto driver

"My grandmother used to save up gold, my mom did as well, and that's actually what paid for my wedding and my education."

— Josef Chylathurey

— Yugandhar

"If they have a daughter, they buy gold for the child's safety. They need the gold for the girl's marriage anyway."

— Padmavathi
